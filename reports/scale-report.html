logos-sort — time against n

Every chart zooms and pans. Ctrl + scroll, or a pinch, zooms around the pointer; a sideways drag picks a range of n; a double-click goes back. The box by the pointer lists every sort at that n, fastest first, with its time relative to logos.

every gesture, key and button
  • Zoom. Ctrl + scroll or pinch, around the pointer. Drag sideways for a range of n, up or down for a range of values, a box for both. Over the value axis, Ctrl + scroll zooms that axis alone. The − and + buttons of a panel zoom around its middle.
  • Plain scroll moves the page, because the page is mostly charts. Click a chart and plain scroll zooms that chart until the pointer leaves it.
  • Move along n. Shift + scroll, a sideways scroll, Shift + drag, the middle button; or a plain drag once “drag pans” is on. On a touch screen: one finger sideways, once zoomed.
  • Go back. Double-click, the 0 key or the panel’s reset button. “reset all” also brings back every hidden series.
  • Keyboard. Tab to a chart; the arrows move along n (Shift for bigger steps), + and − zoom, 0 resets, Esc lets go.
  • Series. Click a legend row to hide one, Ctrl + click to show it alone. The value axis refits to what is in view.
  • Panels. The two charts of a panel share their n window and their pointer. “expand” gives a panel the full width. The bar above stays in view and jumps to any shape.

The dashed grey curves are O(n) and O(n log n) references anchored at logos's largest measured point, so a series that bends above the O(n log n) curve is growing faster than a comparison sort should. The label after each series name is its empirical exponent, the least-squares slope of log t against log n over n ≥ 100k (below that fixed costs dominate). The single-threaded panel compares sorts that each use one thread; the parallel panel compares sorts that each use all threads. Every output was checked equal to std::sort's on the same data; the minimum of the repetitions is plotted. A line that stops early hit the per-sort time budget.

Each panel holds two charts of the same measurements: time per sort, and below it nanoseconds per element, whose value axis is always linear (only its n axis follows the axis toggle). n = 0 is the cost of one call: it has no per-element value, and it cannot sit on a log axis, so the log view leaves it to the tables. The size buttons cut every chart down to the small end, because a linear n axis puts everything below a million into its first pixels. The last row of the ns-per-element table is the scaling slope read off two points: with c the cost per element, p = 1 + ln(c_hi / c_lo) / ln(n_hi / n_lo), so p = 1 is linear scaling and the percentage beside it is how much dearer one element became from 1M to the largest size. Those two rows are measured minutes apart on a machine whose speed drifts, so read that row as a sketch: make slopebench measures the same number in adjacent pairs, where the drift cancels. Sizes below 10k are timed as batches of distinct inputs sorted back to back, since one such sort is shorter than the clock can resolve and repeating one input would train the branch predictor; the parallel sorts are called with all threads there too, so what they show at small n is what such a call costs. That change of method is a SEAM, and the dashed line marks it. Three things flip there at once, all favouring the right-hand side: the working set (a batch is about 2 MB and exceeds L2, one array at n = 10k is 78 KB and does not), the branch history (every batch instance is a different input; the single array is the same input every rep), and the statistic (a batch's total divided by its count, against the minimum of several reps). So a curve may be read along either side of the line, but a step ACROSS it is partly the method. The batch ÷ single row in the ns-per-element table measures that step for this panel: the same n timed both ways, at the one size where both modes resolve. Use it to tell the two apart. Where it is well above 1 a jump at the line is the harness; the sequential contenders sit around 2-4x. Where it is near 1 and the chart still jumps, the jump is real and belongs to the data - a shape is not the same shape at 3162 as at 10000, since the number of runs, cycles or distinct values in it moves with n, and the engine's routing gates are ratios of n. Both happen in this report. The extreme values, tens to hundreds, are all __gnu_parallel::sort at P threads, and they are not a measurement fault but the point restated: a batch calls the sort 26 times back to back, so 26 parallel regions are opened and joined on 10k elements, where the single measurement opens one and keeps the best of five. Everything that panel plots LEFT of the line for a parallel contender is dominated by that per-call cost rather than by sorting, which is what "the parallel sorts are called with all threads there too" means in practice. The circle around a point is memory: the peak heap of one COLD call at that size, in bytes per element, measured in a pass of its own after all the timing (releasing the pools is what makes a call cold, and doing that between timed rows would have left the next row paying for its buffers). The largest reading anywhere in the report is 30 px across and every other scales linearly by it, so a circle means the same number in every panel; with a linear diameter, twice the memory draws four times the ink, so read them as ranks rather than areas. No circle means nothing was allocated - std::sort and the strict in-place entry draw none at any size - and sizes below 10k have none because a size there is a BATCH of distinct inputs, where a peak would be the batch's and not one sort's. Per element, not absolute: absolute bytes would simply grow with n and say nothing, and this way the small-n rows show the fixed pool costs before they amortize.
